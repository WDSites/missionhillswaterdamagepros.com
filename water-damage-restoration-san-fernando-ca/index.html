The city is an enclave of about 2.37 square miles, surrounded by the City of Los Angeles, and Mission Hills lies on its west side. Primary ZIP for this page is 91340. Say your cross streets when you call, because many addresses near the boundary look similar on a map.

Know your city before you call anyone

San Fernando incorporated in 1911 and produces, treats and sells its own water, instead of buying from the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power. If water in your street is the problem, contact the City of San Fernando Public Works. A few blocks west, the same call would go to LADWP.

The same split applies to permits. San Fernando has its own Building and Safety division at City Hall, 117 Macneil Street, and it reviews plans and inspects permitted work. Ask that office before a larger rebuild, because rules vary by the kind of work and the city recommends submitting applications through its online permit counter.

Places that anchor San Fernando

San Fernando Regional Park, at 208 Park Avenue, and the San Fernando Public Library on North Maclay Avenue are well-known reference points, and so is the Casa de Lopez Adobe at 1100 Pico Street. The historic mission is in neighboring Mission Hills, a reminder of how closely the two places are tied. Cross streets matter more than landmarks, but a landmark helps a crew find the right block.

The history of San Fernando runs back to 1874, when Charles Maclay bought about 56,000 acres of the former rancho. A building from that period, or from any older era, may have older plumbing, so say if yours is much older than the homes around it.

Stormwater and the park project

The city has built a rainwater infiltration system at San Fernando Regional Park, an $11.2 million project that began construction on April 4, 2022. It captures runoff from about 940 acres, roughly 70% of the city, to protect the Pacoima Wash and the Los Angeles River and to recharge groundwater. Projects like this reduce street runoff, but they do not change your own flood risk.

To understand yours, check the FEMA flood map for your address and ask the city. Standard homeowners insurance does not pay for flood damage, so a separate flood policy matters if your property is at risk. Water that enters from outside is handled on the flood damage restoration page.
